## 分节 3

ABSTRACT
Unconventional art and Economies of Support
By
Giulia Trabaldo Togna

The term “Unconventional art” aims to describe a practice that happens outside of
the conventional market for artworks. Here, the artists Thomas Hirschhorn, Tino
Sehgal and Jenny Holzer are taken as case studies to prove and explain how the
artworld adapts to support the production and consumption of pieces that work
beyond the marketplace.
Thus, the research draws, through the mapping of different dynamics and actors, a
system defined as Economies of Support, meaning the roles and contributions of
different subjects that ultimately make possible the existence of Unconventional art.

INTRODUCTION. Historical overview
The term “Unconventional art” aims to describe contemporary art practices
generating works that are produced and consumed outside conventional marketplaces
and institutions, and according to specific logics that are here identified as Economies
of Support. While the meaning and qualities of the term “Economies of Support” will
be discussed in the following chapters, this part will focus on the meaning and
definition of “Unconventional art”, chronologically including the fundamental steps
in the recent history of art, with regard to the subject.
In order to define “Unconventional art” this chapter will go through an historical
overview of the landmark events that revolutionized the way in which art was
conceived before the 1920s. Thus, the following part will examine all of those
movements that contributed to build new boundaries not only in the way art was
produced, but also in the way it entered into consumption.
The first significant event with regard to the definition of “unconventional” is
represented by Marcel Duchamp’s submission of a urinal, titled “Fountain” (fig 1.), to
the Society of Independent Artists in 1917. In fact, this gesture marked the emergence
of Readymade, which put at stake the recognition of the object as art and, therefore,
all the conventions within the artworld itself. Despite the complexity of Duchamp’s
legacy, “there is no question that the history of modernism and the evolution of art
today are unimaginable without him. By blurring the boundary between art and life,
Duchamp set at stage for much of what was to come”1. Undoubtedly, this moment
represents the very beginning of a radical change that will allow for much of what
