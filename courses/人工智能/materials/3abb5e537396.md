Hybrid Clinical Framework: Multimodal Large
Language Models Integration with Proprietary
Diagnostic Engines
Guokai Zhang, Keke Dou, Jianfeng Hou, Cong Liu, and Yiqun Lin

Abstract—Current medical Visual Question Answering (VQA)
systems face inherent limitations: traditional models exhibit constrained generalizability owing to their reliance on small, domainspecific training datasets, while state-of-the-art Multimodal Large
Language Models (MLLMs), despite their impressive capabilities,
often lack the requisite depth of medical domain knowledge,
potentially resulting in factual inaccuracies and hallucinations.
To address these challenges, we introduce HyCF, a hybrid clinical
framework designed to enhance reliability through the synergistic
integration of MLLMs’ broad reasoning capabilities with the
precision of specialized domain-specific models. Our approach
employs a Chain-of-Thought decomposition process to systematically parse complex queries into a hierarchical Intent Tree
comprising discrete subtasks. These subtasks are subsequently
processed by dedicated specialized models, with MLLMs synthesizing the intermediate outputs into coherent, comprehensive
responses. To assess the framework’s capacity for complex compositional reasoning and multi-intent query resolution, we developed MIM-VQA (https://ieee-dataport.org/documents/mim-vqa),
a novel dataset constructed through clinician-curated templates
leveraging GPT-4, with stringent filtering protocols ensuring
clinical fidelity and real-world relevance. Comprehensive evaluation on both public MIM-VQA and the established VQARAD benchmark demonstrates that our method achieves stateof-the-art performance in zero-shot medical VQA, establishing
its potential for advancing clinical decision support systems.
Index Terms—Visual Question Answering, Multimodal Large
Language Models, Agent, Medical Imaging

to answer a natural language question about a given image,
requiring a synergistic understanding of both computer vision
and natural language processing [2]–[4]. A specialized and
high-impact subfield, medical VQA, applies this paradigm
to the healthcare domain, aiming to create systems that can
answer clinical questions based on medical imagery such as Xrays, Computed Tomography (CT), and Magnetic Resonance
Imaging (MRI) [5], [6]. The primary goal of medical VQA

Previous medical VQA methods are primarily built on
a multimodal encoder-decoder framework [10]–[15]. This
structure involved a multimodal encoder to process inputs,
where image features were extracted by convolutional neural

medical applications, particularly medical VQA [21]–[24],
has gained significant attention. Specifically, some studies
have attempted to directly apply general MLLMs, like BLIP2, and LLaVA [18], [25], to medical VQA tasks involving

medical adaptations still face limitations [26]–[28]. The scale
and diversity of available medical data remain limited, leading

To address the above limitations, we propose a novel hybrid
clinical framework named HyCF, which integrates MLLMs
with proprietary models specifically designed for real-world
medical image diagnosis. The core idea is to leverage Chainof-Thought reasoning (CoT) [29] to decompose complex user
queries into manageable subqueries, each linked to a proprietary clinical intent. Based on this decomposition, we construct
an Intent Tree, where each node corresponds to a clinical
subtask executed by a dedicated proprietary model optimized
for that specific function. To efficiently manage multiple
simultaneous intents, we design a high-performance matching
algorithm that aligns detected intents with the nodes of the
Intent Tree, thereby determining the optimal execution path.
This strategy facilitates rational allocation of computational
resources and specialized models, significantly improving processing efficiency and system scalability. Finally, the intermediate results generated by proprietary models corresponding
to different detected intents, together with the original input,
are fed into the MLLMs. Leveraging its powerful integrative
and reasoning capabilities, HyCF synthesizes a coherent and
comprehensive final diagnostic output. This hybrid framework
not only overcomes the limitations of proprietary models in
handling complex multimodal tasks but also fully exploits the
strengths of MLLMs in semantic understanding and problem
solving, substantially enhancing diagnostic accuracy and reliability in clinical practice.
We validated our proposed approach across various medical
VQA datasets, including a public dataset, VQA-RAD [30], and
our collected MIM-VQA (https://ieee-dataport.org/documents/
mim-vqa). Existing medical VQA datasets, such as VQARAD, suffer from two main shortcomings: (1) their coverage
of preset clinical intents is relatively vague, making it difficult
to accurately evaluate a model’s intent recognition ability;
and (2) they lack effective means to assess the intervention
effect of proprietary models. To address these limitations, we
constructed MIM-VQA, which anchors preset intents with
higher precision and introduces samples containing multiple
interwoven intents. These characteristics make the dataset
closer to real clinical scenarios and provide a more effective
benchmark for evaluating the multi-intent processing capability of our framework. Specifically, MIM-VQA was designed
with clinician-curated templates and generated via GPT-4, followed by rigorous filtering based on faithfulness and relevance
metrics, to better assess compositional reasoning and intentaware natural language generation—capabilities that cannot be
adequately evaluated by VQA-RAD or other existing medical
VQA datasets. Experimental results demonstrate that HyCF

surpasses fully supervised baselines in zero-shot settings,
achieving state-of-the-art performance and significantly enhancing the adaptability of MLLMs in medical domains.

II. R ELATED W ORK
A. Proprietary Models in Medical Domains
Deep learning has advanced medical image analysis, enabling accurate segmentation, detection, and classification
across modalities such as CT, MRI, and ultrasound. CNN
architectures like U-Net [31] excel at segmenting organs,
tumors, and vessels, while ResNet [32] and EfficientNet [33]
effectively classify pulmonary, hepatic, and dermatological
diseases. Publicly available APIs have also demonstrated
strong performance tasks including chest [34], retinal [35],
and skin [36] disease classification, as well as lesion and
tumor detection [37], [38]. Although these proprietary models
automate feature extraction and support clinical decisionmaking with efficient inference and robust accuracy, their
focus on single modalities limits effectiveness in complex
multimodal diagnostic scenarios.
B. Traditional Medical VQA
Traditional medical VQA methods typically use multimodal
encoder-decoder frameworks, where CNNs or vision transformers extract image features and RNNs or transformerbased models encode questions [4], [39], [40]. The combined
features are then passed to classifiers or text decoders to
generate answers. Notably, datasets like VQA-RAD and associated baselines [30] serve as key benchmarks. However,
these approaches depend heavily on limited, domain-specific
datasets such as VQA-RAD and PathVQA [41], cover only
a narrow range of diseases and imaging modalities. Consequently, their generalization is poor, hindering adaptation
to multimodal, multi-disease scenarios typical of real-world
clinical environments [42].
Moreover, traditional methods struggle with complex medical reasoning, which demands multi-step inference, causal
understanding, and fine-grained semantic fusion across modalities. They rarely leverage external medical knowledge bases
like UMLS or SNOMED CT for enhanced reasoning, limiting

their effectiveness in clinical decision support [43]. Additionally, the heterogeneity of multimodal data—such as X-rays,
CT, MRI, and clinical texts—poses challenges for cross-modal
representation learning, where conventional fusion techniques
often fail to capture rich medical semantics.
C. MLLMs in Medical Domains
In recent years, MLLMs have advanced significantly in
their ability to simultaneously understand language and vision, becoming foundational tools in related research fields.
Notable examples include GPT-4o [44], which processes text,
image, and audio inputs to enable real-time multimodal interactions; Doubao-1.5-pro [45], which leverages a visual
deep thinking framework to support high-resolution inputs for
detailed visual comprehension; Qwen3 [46], recognized for its
strengths in Chinese semantic understanding and multilingual
multimodal capabilities; and Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet [47],
distinguished by its fast performance and expertise in visual reasoning tasks such as chart and graph interpretation.
Building on these advances, several studies have investigated
the direct application of general-purpose MLLMs to medical
domains, particularly in visual question answering (VQA)
for radiology and pathology, with early examples including
BLIP-2 [18] and LLaVA [25]. However, due to the lack of
domain-specific medical knowledge, these models are prone to
hallucinations in clinical image interpretation, which adversely
affects the accuracy and reliability of the results. To address
the limitations of general MLLMs in medical applications,
various medical adaptation approaches have been proposed.
A typical example is LLaVA-Med [21], which fine-tunes
LLaVA on large-scale medical image-text datasets, effectively
injecting clinical knowledge and significantly improving the
model’s comprehension of medical images and texts.
Furthermore, some studies enhance medical knowledge
acquisition and reasoning by integrating knowledge graphs,
retrieval augmentation, and external reasoning tools. However,
medical adaptation models still face key challenges: limited
and insufficiently diverse datasets hinder generalization, domain adaptation cannot fully remove biases from general
pretraining, reasoning processes often lack transparency, and
unresolved hallucinations continue to pose risks to clinical
safety [26]–[28].

1.) Medical VQA
For the medical visual question answering (Medical VQA)
task, we follow prior studies [51]–[54] and employ four
widely adopted evaluation metrics: BLEU [55], ROUGE [56],
GLEU [57], and METEOR [58]. Specifically, BLEU evaluates the precision of overlapping n-grams between generated
and reference answers with a brevity penalty to discourage
overly short responses. ROUGE measures the overlap of ngrams and sequences, with ROUGE-L further assessing the
longest common subsequence to capture semantic and structural similarity. GLEU balances precision and recall by taking
their minimum, offering a fairer evaluation for generation
tasks. METEOR computes a harmonic mean of unigram precision and recall, while introducing a fragmentation penalty to
account for disjoint alignments. Overall, higher scores across
these metrics indicate superior generation quality.

2) Roles of Chain-of-Thought and Intent Tree: We conducted ablation studies on four tasks to evaluate the contributions of the Intent Tree (IT) and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) modules in Table V. Across all tasks, the full model consistently
outperformed ablated variants. Removing IT led to substantial
performance drops, highlighting its essential role in hierarchical intent organization and multi-path reasoning. CoT removal
caused moderate declines, indicating its importance for logical
coherence and detailed content generation. Specifically, IT was
critical for higher-order BLEU and ROUGE metrics in DI and
MT, while CoT enhanced semantic and logical consistency
across all tasks. Overall, the synergy of IT and CoT enables
HyCF to achieve robust, high-quality performance in intent
recognition and generation.
3) Effect of Intent Execution Path Optimization: We evaluated the effect of Intent Execution Path Optimization (IEPO)
on intent path retrieval across four tasks using 1,020 VQA
pairs. Figure 4 shows the cumulative distribution function
(CDF) of execution time, comparing w/ IEPO and traditional
depth-first search (DFS) algorithm (w/o IEPO). For single-

intent tasks (AL, DI, DD), both methods show nearly identical
CDFs, as deterministic execution paths leave little room for
optimization. In contrast, for multi-intent tasks (MT), IEPO
shifts the CDF leftward, indicating faster completion. The
improvement stems from IEPO’s ability to exploit overlapping
execution segments, reducing redundant computations. These
results highlight that IEPO significantly enhances efficiency in
complex, multi-intent scenarios, while its impact on simpler,
single-intent tasks is minimal.
4) Summary: In summary, the ablation experiments thoroughly demonstrate the critical roles of the hierarchical intent
tree structure and Chain-of-Thought reasoning module in enhancing HyCF’s generation quality and reasoning capabilities.
Meanwhile, the intent execution path optimization strategy is
indispensable for improving time efficiency in complex multiintent tasks. Their synergistic effects significantly bolster the
robustness and scalability of our multi-task medical intelligent
agent system.
C. Case Study
To provide a clear comparison of response quality between
different models, we present the results of the same user

offers targeted treatment options, making it more suitable for
professional diagnosis.

construct sophisticated intent tree structures combined with
Intent Execution Path Optimization to achieve accurate capture
and positioning of intent nodes. This design organically integrates the intent node outputs with initial input information to
