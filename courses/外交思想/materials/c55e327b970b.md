案例名称：
An Intercultural Conflict Case - Teamwork With Korean
Case Description
In November 2018, during the World Youth Innovation Camp at National Tsing Hua University in Taiwan, I partnered with Kim Joon from Busan International High School on a "Smart Sports Gear" project. He is the oldest team member in the grade here. At the beginning of our cooperation, he asked me about my age. I said I was a junior high school student. He was quite surprised, but still told me in Korean English that he was glad to meet me. Therefore, I also think he is a relatively friendly big brother and doesn't feel timid because of the age gap.
By the third night, tensions simmered as we worked alone in the lab debugging a heart rate sensor—a setup ripe for collision. I blurted out while staring at the screen: "Your transistor bias voltage is wrong." Joon, hunched over soldering a sensor, jerked his soldering iron and burned his finger. Cursing under his breath in Korean, he glared as I shoved the oscilloscope toward him: "Look at this waveform! It's clipping—fix the circuit or tomorrow's demo will crash!"
His temple throbbed. In Korean hierarchy, a junior like me should’ve wrapped criticism in honorifics, not stabbed the screen with commands. He ripped off his anti-static wristband and slammed it down: "Do all Chinese people talk like you?!"I was so intimidated by his arrogance that I didn't choose to retort. I originally thought this was a discussion that often occurs in the laboratory, but he obviously didn't accept it. I don't know if I should apologize, so our discussion ended so awkwardly
For two hours, the lab echoed with hostile silence. I angrily rewrote the code, hammering the keyboard; he recklessly resoldered transistors, cranking the iron’s heat. At zero o'clock
, my stomach growled—we'd skipped dinner.
The snack cabinet held only one pack of Shin Ramyun. As I cracked the dry noodle block, Joon muttered without looking up: "Hey, Koreans usually add eggs to ramyun." Before I could react, he pulled a spiced corned egg from his toolkit—a hometown treasure from Busan. We boiled the noodles in a beaker over a hotplate, the stench of burnt solder blending with spicy broth. Tension dissolved like steam.​
With the broth gone, Joon sketched a wobbly waveform on a circuit board with his iron: " Actually, I noticed voltage drifting this afternoon." He avoided eye contact, fingers tracing his uniform’s embroidered Hangul. "But you guys just “bang” throw mistakes like bombs into kimchi stew."
It hit me then—at his Korean tech school, fixes were folded into paper cranes and handed privately to seniors. My public "It’s wrong!" had been a Kakao group chat humiliation.
At last, we found a compromise: he recalculated the bias voltage while I tweaked software filters to mask residual errors. The soldering iron passed between us like a baton. Somewhere between "you" and "me," we became "we"—
The heart rate module finally stabilized. Joon suddenly said in stiff Chinese: "You Chinese ,are like this." He held up a multimeter with probes bent at right angles. "Fast and straight." I tossed him half a chocolate bar: "You Koreans, are like heat-shrink tubing." Pointing to his bench, where plastic sleeves tightened under heat. "You curl up when scolded, but get stronger when warmed."
We didn't win the trophy at last, but the night before leaving Taiwan, Joon stuffed a seaweed snack into my luggage—inside was a charred circuit board fragment. Scorched onto it: that clipped waveform, and three jagged Chinese characters: 谢谢你.

AnalysisA.  Major Issues & Theoretical Explanation
The conflict stemmed from contrasting cultural norms in delivering criticism. 
Korean: Hierarchical structures expect juniors to use indirect, honorific-laden language when pointing out errors. Public correction can be perceived as humiliation. 
Chinese: Direct, efficiency-focused feedback is often valued in technical settings, with less emphasis on hierarchical decorum.
Joon interpreted the narrator’s blunt "Your transistor bias voltage is wrong" as disrespectful, triggering defensiveness.

Age/seniority gaps (high schooler vs. junior high student) complicated teamwork in a culture (Korean) where age dictates interaction norms.
Joon assumed the narrator’s directness was a "Chinese" trait. The narrator perceived Joon’s reaction as "arrogance" rather than a cultural mismatch.
Joon’s expectation of deference clashed with the narrator’s task-oriented focus, creating friction.

Conflict Resolution Strategies
Both parties retreated into passive-aggressive silence (working separately, hostile body language).
Shared vulnerability (hunger, a humble meal) and symbolic gestures (ramyun, the multimeter analogy) allowed non-verbal reconciliation.
Informal, humanizing moments can bridge divides where formal communication fails.

Theoretical explanation: 
In analyzing the cross-cultural conflict between the Chinese student and Kim Joon, several intercultural communication theories can be applied to understand the cultural dynamics at play. The differences in communication styles, power structures, and face-related behaviors are key to understanding the tension and how it was eventually resolved.
The Chinese student comes from a collectivist culture, where the emphasis is on group harmony and cooperation. In collectivist societies like China, individuals are more likely to consider the face of others, maintaining relationships and avoiding open confrontation. This is reflected in the student’s reaction to the accommodation conditions. When the situation was explained to him, he chose not to point out the issues with the living conditions. He valued maintaining a good relationship with others, as well as avoiding unnecessary conflict. This approach aligns with face theory, which suggests that individuals from collectivist cultures are more concerned with maintaining face and harmony, even if it means tolerating discomfort or avoiding direct confrontation. The student’s behavior—choosing to tolerate the substandard conditions and avoiding direct criticism—was a way to protect both his face and the face of others involved.
On the other hand, Joon, coming from Korean culture, which also places importance on collectivism but with a stronger emphasis on hierarchy and respect for authority, reacted quite differently. While both China and Korea are collectivist societies, Korea’s emphasis on power distance means there is a clear hierarchical structure in social interactions, and this hierarchy influences how criticism is given and received. Joon, being the senior member of the team, expected a more respectful, indirect approach when receiving feedback, especially in a collaborative environment. In Korean culture, criticism, particularly from someone junior, should be delivered with respect, often using indirect language and honorifics. The directness of the Chinese student’s critique, delivered without the expected level of deference, was perceived by Joon as a threat to his face. This conflict was further compounded by the differences in high-context vs. low-context communication styles. While Chinese communication is often indirect and relies on context, Joon, influenced by Korean norms, expected a more formal and respectful approach to criticism.
Joon’s reaction, where he accused the Chinese student of speaking too bluntly and criticized the way Chinese people communicate, highlights his discomfort with the directness. His behavior reflects a misunderstanding of the Chinese approach, which is influenced by a desire to solve problems efficiently. In collectivist cultures, the goal is often to maintain group harmony, but in Joon’s case, the lack of indirectness and formality made him feel disrespected, thus triggering a response that was defensive and angry.
Despite these differences, both individuals found a way to compromise and adjust. Joon recalculated the bias voltage while the Chinese student adjusted the software, and they managed to stabilize the heart rate sensor. Their ability to find a middle ground demonstrates the potential for collaboration once they recognized the cultural differences that influenced their behavior. By the end of the interaction, Joon made a comment about how Chinese people are "fast and straight," while the Chinese student responded by comparing Koreans to "heat-shrink tubing," a playful acknowledgment of the different cultural ways they approach challenges.
This conflict, shaped by cultural differences in power distance, face negotiation, and communication style, shows how deeply ingrained cultural values can influence interactions. It also highlights the importance of understanding these cultural dynamics to avoid misunderstandings and find ways to collaborate effectively.

Process Outline:
Premise: Both parties involved in the conflict must avoid escalating the situation to a destructive level (e.g., threats, coercion, or deception). Instead, the focus should be on turning the conflict into a productive interaction, aiming for cooperation. Both should approach the conflict with an open mind, striving for mutual understanding and respect, avoiding any cultural stereotypes, and considering the context.
Recognize the Conflict: The resolution process begins with a clear understanding of the context, including social, cultural, and personal factors that may be influencing the situation. Both the Chinese student and the other party involved should analyze the conflict, identify the root causes, and define the specific issues at hand.
Understand Oneself: The Chinese student needs to reflect on his conflict style. In the case, he leans towards the "Avoiding" and "Compromising" styles, while his counterparts may be exhibiting a "Dominating" style. It is important for the Chinese student to recognize the benefits of adjusting his approach, while also understanding the need for his counterparts to soften their dominating style.
Understand the Other Party: A key element of resolving intercultural conflicts is mindful listening. By carefully listening to both verbal and non-verbal communication, the Chinese student can better understand his counterpart's perspective and feelings. This understanding can foster cultural empathy, which in turn will allow for more thoughtful, respectful, and appropriate responses.
Maintain Contact: Effective communication through dialogue is crucial. The Chinese student and the other party should engage in peaceful conversations, striving for a deeper understanding of each other’s cultural contexts and personal experiences. Continuous dialogue can help to bridge misunderstandings and encourage cooperation.

Detailed Resolution:
In the conflict with Kim Joon, the Chinese student found himself in a challenging position due to the clash between his direct communication style and the Korean hierarchical expectations. The tension escalated when the student bluntly pointed out a technical error, which Joon perceived as disrespectful. To resolve this, the student should first recognize the cultural underpinnings of Joon’s reaction—specifically, the Korean emphasis on hierarchy and indirect communication. By acknowledging these differences, the student can adjust his approach to align more closely with Korean norms.
For instance, he could preface criticism with phrases like, “I might be wrong, but could we check this part together?” This softer approach preserves Joon’s face while still addressing the technical issue. Additionally, the student could seek to understand Joon’s perspective by asking open-ended questions, such as, “How would you typically handle feedback in your school?” This fosters mutual respect and opens a dialogue about cultural expectations.
The resolution of this conflict was ultimately achieved through informal bonding, such as sharing ramyun and exchanging metaphorical compliments. These actions helped bridge the cultural gap by creating a personal connection outside the formal team setting. The student’s willingness to adapt his communication style, combined with Joon’s gradual acceptance of their differences, allowed them to collaborate effectively. This case highlights the importance of flexibility and empathy in intercultural interactions. By being mindful of hierarchical norms and adjusting his directness, the student not only resolved the immediate conflict but also built a stronger working relationship with Joon.
In conclusion, this conflict underscores the need for individuals to balance their natural communication styles with cultural sensitivity. While directness may be efficient in some contexts, it can clash with cultures that prioritize hierarchy and indirectness. The key takeaway is that successful intercultural collaboration requires both parties to step outside their comfort zones, adapt their behaviors, and find common ground through shared experiences. By doing so, they can transform potential conflicts into opportunities for mutual understanding and growth, ultimately achieving the goal of “harmony in diversity.”
